* Overview

Given a body position with mediapipe coordinates -- synthetic, combinatorially
enumerated or inferred from video -- map them to tracking points to estimate the
level of effort required to hold the pose.

+ gradually explore the domain of possible poses by starting with many
  constraints and relaxing them, freeing other parts of the simulated model to
  move.
  - discover the topology of movement of one arm, without approximating the
    human joints. then gradually add overlapping subsets of joints. randomly
    explore reachable surfaces of the manifold of human poses.
  - gradually include actual translation or free movement.
+ maybe reinforcement learning using computational geometry metrics as a proxy
  for energy -- e.g. use metrics like displaced volume/mass over motion arcs as
  a proxy for energy to develop KinematicNet sequences
  - not training motion patterns or tasks (at first)... just develop a "map" of
    the topology of movement. I'm sure this has been overdone by this point, but
    idk if it's done like this. maybe.

Understanding how this works should develop insight into differences between
human musculoskeletal design and whatever the robotic equivalent is.

+ The physical form that evolution produced can endure a lot more volatility...
  with some constraints
+ Typical robot designs are highly linear. Relatively speaking, electric motors
  and engineering design patterns are difficult to extend & to make more
  flexible (e.g. "just add another motor" or whatever could totally overwhelm
  the local strain tolerance ... if you can even fit it in).
+ It's "implicitly" assumed that programming motion for robots whose kinematics
  are composed of "more linear" units -- like the joints & connections in a
  kinematic chain -- would be simpler.
  - however, (I think) human & animal nervous systems benefit from some kind of
    "convexity", simplifying their motor coordination
    - reduced computational hardness
    - redundancy & ability to encounter volatility (variance in variance in
      their environments)

Think about the differences between electricity and hydrodynamics:

+ in/compressible fluids require design of surfaces to accomodate volumes, in
  order to produce specific effects
+ electricity moves through components is like "2.5D". For some applications,
  designs are nano-scopic & essentially 2-D
  - (i don't have time to explain this)
  - this is basically ignoring magnetism (and it's super-oversimplified)

To compare robotics vs animal physiology:

+ Typical mechanical engineering designs move power through the interior (e.g.
  gears _in_ a box; shaft in a motor). Wiring goes on the outside, but that's
  problematic if it tangles.
+ With animal physiology, the power is transmitted on the outside (flexors &
  extensors). we have an endoskeleton. But crucially, the muscles/tendons
  connect to _surfaces_. These are often described by analogy to an enumeration of
  motion without constraint

| revolute | prismatic | cylindrical | spherical | universal |
